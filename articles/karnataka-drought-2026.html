Karnataka's 2026 Drought: What Happened, and Why

By late October 2026, Karnataka had declared 226 of its 240 taluks drought-affected — close to 95% of the state — and put kharif losses above ₹62,000 crore. Here's the data behind the headline, and the five reasons it got this bad.

October 2026 · 7 min read

The scale, in numbers

Karnataka's southwest monsoon (June–September) closed at roughly 35% below normal — the state received about 551mm against a normal of 852mm. That headline number was itself the endpoint of a season that kept getting worse, not better: June ran a 42% deficit, the fourth-driest June in 50 years; July eased slightly to 29%; August narrowed further to 23%; and then September — the month that typically helps top up reservoirs before winter — collapsed to a 54% deficit, the worst of any month in the season.

The shortfall wasn't even across the state. South Interior and North Interior Karnataka each ran roughly 41–42% below normal, while coastal Karnataka fared comparatively better at around 28%. At the district level, Ramanagara recorded an 87% rainfall deficit — the worst in the state — and the Cauvery catchment districts of Kodagu, Mysuru, and Chikkamagaluru (down 45%, 58%, and 42% respectively) were especially hard hit, with direct consequences for reservoirs downstream.

Karnataka's drought-hit taluk count grew in four documented stages: 101 taluks on August 27 (89 of them severe), 124 by September 17, 217 — over 90% of the state — by October 3, and 226 by mid-to-late October.

The economic toll climbed alongside it. In a letter to the Prime Minister dated October 22, 2026, Deputy Chief Minister G. Parameshwara put total kharif losses at ₹62,481.88 crore across 57.93 lakh hectares, and formally sought ₹20,585.73 crore in National Disaster Response Fund assistance. That figure had already climbed from ₹46,324.5 crore across 47.09 lakh hectares just weeks earlier, in early October. Tur, maize, cotton, sugarcane, soybean, and green gram were among the worst-hit field crops, alongside onion, arecanut, and red chilli. Sowing itself never recovered from a bad start — by the first week of July, only 34% of the seasonal target had been met.

Reservoirs tell the same story faster than the rainfall numbers alone. By late July, Karnataka's 14 major reservoirs held a combined ~405 tmc ft — about 45% of capacity, nearly 42% below the same date a year earlier. The Krishna Raja Sagar (KRS) dam held about 17 tmc ft against a full capacity of 49.45 tmc ft, its lowest storage in six years, and the Kabini reservoir's usable live storage had effectively reached zero by September. On September 8, Karnataka cut its combined Cauvery release to Tamil Nadu by roughly 57%, to 5,200 cusecs, citing poor inflows rather than a refusal to share. The Krishna basin, helped by rainfall further north in Maharashtra, fared noticeably better — the Almatti dam was actually holding slightly more water in 2026 than on the same date in 2025, a reminder of how unevenly this deficit landed even within one state.

The reasons

1. El Niño weakened the monsoon at its source. Roughly 60% of El Niño years between 1951 and 2022 produced a below-average Indian monsoon. The India Meteorological Department had forecast below-normal 2026 southwest-monsoon rainfall specifically because of strengthening El Niño conditions — a forecast Chief Minister D.K. Shivakumar cited directly when he asked the Prime Minister for a central assessment team in mid-July. Karnataka's government, citing INCOIS forecasts, has said El Niño conditions were expected to persist into May 2027.

2. India's monsoon as a whole was unusually weak — and Karnataka fell well short even of that. The national 2026 southwest monsoon closed about 12.6% below average, India's weakest in over a decade, with an IMD official attributing the shortfall largely to El Niño. Karnataka's own deficit — 35% — was nearly three times the national figure, pointing to a regional amplification on top of the broader El Niño effect, consistent with the state's known vulnerability in its interior and Malnad catchment regions.

3. The rain that did fall came in the wrong pattern, not just the wrong quantity. Multiple reports from mid-season described sudden, intense bursts followed by long dry spells, rather than the sustained, well-distributed rainfall that recharges soil moisture and reservoirs effectively. A monthly total delivered as a handful of intense days does far less for agriculture and groundwater recharge than the same total spread across the month — part of why reservoir storage fell so much faster than the headline deficit numbers alone would suggest.

4. September's collapse undercut the season's only real recovery window. The monsoon's retreat phase in September is often when late-season rain tops up reservoirs ahead of the dry months. Instead, September recorded a 54% deficit — the worst of any month in the season — removing the one remaining chance for storage levels to recover before winter.

5. Heat compounded the water shortage. Rising temperatures were reported alongside the rainfall deficit, with Kalaburagi recording a maximum of 38°C during the dry spell. Higher temperatures increase both evaporative losses from already-depleted reservoirs and water demand for irrigation and drinking supply, compounding a shortfall that was already severe on the supply side alone.

A severe, worsening, officially acknowledged drought — even where the exact numbers used to describe it don't yet agree with each other.

A note on the numbers that don't agree

Official and media accounts of this drought don't fully agree with each other, and that's worth being upfront about rather than smoothing over. Karnataka's deputy chief minister has at different points compared 2026 to the state's last comparable drought in 1976, and separately described the 2026 monsoon as the second-lowest since 1956; other reporting has called it the weakest since 1918 or framed it against a different multi-decade baseline. The taluk count similarly moved in uneven jumps — 101, then 124, then 217, then 226 — as successive assessment rounds added districts rather than in one clean count, and the final NDRF request describes the 226 total as "177 declared earlier plus 49 additional," a breakdown that doesn't reconcile cleanly with the 217-taluk figure reported in early October. None of this changes the overall picture. But a reader looking for one single authoritative number for "how bad" or "how it compares historically" won't find one yet — the state's own figures were still being revised as this was written.

Sources: Deccan Chronicle, Deccan Herald, NewsKarnataka, The Week, Global Agriculture / New Kerala, ETV Bharat, The Print, Siasat, and Karnataka government correspondence to the Prime Minister's Office (via the news coverage citing it), all reporting between June and October 2026. This article reflects the information available as of late October 2026; the state's loss estimates and NDRF request were still being processed by the Centre at the time of writing.
